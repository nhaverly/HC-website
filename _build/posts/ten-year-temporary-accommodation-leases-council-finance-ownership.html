Councils are being urged to swap nightly paid temporary accommodation for leases of ten years or more. On cost, the case is strong. But a long lease is a long-term liability, and when it ends the council usually owns nothing.

Temporary is now permanent

On 10 November Inside Housing published a comment piece from a temporary accommodation provider urging councils to stop procuring for short stays and start procuring for the long term. Families now stay in TA for months or years, and short-term procurement no longer fits.

I agree with the diagnosis.

MHCLG figures show 135,580 households in temporary accommodation in England on 31 March 2026, a record, including 177,530 children. Of those households, 51,390 were in nightly paid self-contained accommodation. That is 37.9% of the total, up 2.3 percentage points in a year.

Councils spent a record £2.93bn on temporary accommodation in 2025-26, Inside Housing reported in September. That is 88% more than in 2020-21.

The next quarterly figures are due on 26 November. I do not expect a different story.

The case for going long

Longer leases are spreading. The comment piece cites a lease of more than ten years at Chesham House in Havering, projected to save over £9.7m across the agreement. In April, Brent's Cabinet approved leases of ten years or longer for around 367 units, projecting £4.8m a year in savings against nightly paid accommodation, according to local press reports.

Those are serious savings. Paying nightly rates for a household you know will stay three years means paying for flexibility you will never use.

I am not arguing against long leases. I am arguing that they should be treated for what they are.

A ten-year lease is borrowing by another name

Leases no longer sit quietly in the revenue budget. As CIPFA put it, "implementation of IFRS 16 will be mandatory from 1 April 2024". Any lease of more than 12 months, unless the asset is low value, now brings a right-of-use asset and a matching lease liability onto the balance sheet. That raises the capital financing requirement and has to be reflected in borrowing limits.

So this is not unique to long leases. What changes is scale. A two-year lease adds two years of payments to the balance sheet. Sign a ten-year lease on 50 homes and you have taken on a decade of fixed obligations that look a lot like debt. It deserves the same governance as debt. That means the capital strategy, the prudential indicators and a proper options appraisal, not just a procurement award.

The question nobody asks: what do we own at the end?

This is where my own experience comes in. The 42-year institutional funding structure I led for Westminster City Council acquired 368 homes that had previously been leased for temporary accommodation. It embedded £33.5m of retrofit funding and leaves the council with unencumbered ownership of the homes when the lease ends.

That last point is the whole game. A conventional private sector lease ends with the keys handed back and a fresh deal at whatever the market rent is then. The council has paid for ten years of supply and has nothing on its balance sheet to show for it.

Not every council can do a 42-year deal. But every council signing a long lease should show it has compared the alternatives: buying, institutional funding that ends in ownership, or recycling value from stock it already holds. On that last option, Westminster's £50m, 40-year sale-and-leaseback with the City of Westminster Pension Fund released capital from 124 existing TA homes to fund new supply.

The ten-year line matters for subsidy too

There is a reason so many of these deals are structured as ten years plus one day. DWP's housing benefit subsidy guidance limits subsidy on most leased temporary accommodation to 90% of the January 2011 Local Housing Allowance rate. The District Councils' Network has put the resulting gap at £268m a year for district councils alone.

But in England that cap applies only to accommodation held outside the HRA "on a lease granted for a term not exceeding ten years". Go beyond ten years and the 2011 cap falls away. In practice, that means recovering rent by reference to 100% of current Local Housing Allowance rates, not 90% of rates frozen in 2011.

Councils are already using this. Inside Housing reported in June 2025 that Birmingham was moving leased properties onto terms of ten years and one day, held in the HRA and let at affordable rents of 80% of market rent, recoverable through housing benefit. The council put the gap on its existing block contract at about £7m a year across 957 properties.

That is a significant prize. But note the trade. The same ten-year line that unlocks the better subsidy is what turns the deal into a long-term liability on your balance sheet. You cannot have one without the other, and unlike a nightly placement, you cannot walk away when the arithmetic changes.

Prevention has to count as investment

There is a wider policy opening. The Treasury plans to introduce preventative departmental expenditure limits at the 2027 Spending Review, as Lígia Teixeira of the Centre for Homelessness Impact set out in Inside Housing on 11 November. She notes English councils spent £4bn gross on homelessness services last year.

If the new framework is to mean anything, long-term TA supply that takes families out of nightly paid rooms should count as invest-to-save, not crisis spending.

What this means for councils

  • Treat long leases as capital decisions. Bring them into the capital strategy and prudential indicators, and test them against your borrowing limits before you sign.
  • Always run the ownership comparison. Lease, acquire, institutional funding ending in ownership, or releasing value from existing stock. Compare whole-life cost and the position at the end of the term, not just year-one savings.
  • Read the end of the lease first. Hand-back conditions, break clauses, rent reviews and renewal rights decide what the deal really costs.
  • Check which side of the ten-year line you are on. Leases of ten years or less sit under the 2011 subsidy cap; longer leases fall outside it. Model subsidy for the full term on the right basis.
  • Prepare for the Spending Review. Build the evidence that long-term supply is prevention, because that is the case the new preventative budgets will need.

Moving away from nightly paid accommodation is the right direction. Just make sure the deal you sign today is one your successor will thank you for in ten years.
