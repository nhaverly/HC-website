Councils are being urged to swap nightly paid temporary accommodation for leases of ten years or more. On cost, the case is strong. But a long lease is a long-term liability, and when it ends the council usually owns nothing.

Temporary is now permanent

On 10 November Inside Housing published a comment piece from a temporary accommodation provider urging councils to stop procuring for short stays and start procuring for the long term. The argument: families now stay in TA for months or years, so short leases, nightly paid placements and repeated tenders no longer fit.

I agree with the diagnosis. The numbers leave no room to argue otherwise.

MHCLG figures show 135,580 households in temporary accommodation in England on 31 March 2026, a record, including 177,530 children. Of those households, 51,390 were in nightly paid self-contained accommodation. That is 37.9% of the total, up 2.3 percentage points in a year.

Councils spent a record £2.93bn on temporary accommodation in 2025-26, Inside Housing reported in September. That is 88% more than in 2020-21.

The next quarterly homelessness statistics are due on 26 November. I do not expect them to tell a different story.

The case for going long

Longer leases are spreading. The comment piece cites a lease of more than ten years at Chesham House in Havering, projected to save over £9.7m across the agreement. In April, Brent's Cabinet approved leases of ten years or longer for around 367 units, projecting £4.8m a year in savings against nightly paid accommodation, according to local press reports.

Those are serious savings. If you are paying nightly rates for a household you know will be with you for three years, you are paying a premium for flexibility you will never use.

So I am not here to argue against long leases. I am here to argue that they should be treated for what they are.

A ten-year lease is borrowing by another name

A long lease no longer sits quietly in the revenue budget. As CIPFA put it, "implementation of IFRS 16 will be mandatory from 1 April 2024". Councils must recognise a right-of-use asset and a matching lease liability for most leased-in property. CIPFA also notes that right-of-use assets affect the capital financing requirement, and that lease liabilities should be reflected when setting borrowing limits.

In plain terms: sign a ten-year lease on 50 homes and you have taken on a decade of fixed obligations that look a lot like debt. It deserves the same governance as debt. That means the capital strategy, the prudential indicators and a proper options appraisal, not just a procurement award.

Too often lease decisions are framed as a cheaper version of nightly paid. They are not. They are a different kind of commitment.

The question nobody asks: what do we own at the end?

This is where my own experience comes in. The 42-year institutional funding structure I led for Westminster City Council acquired 368 homes that had previously been leased for temporary accommodation. It embedded £33.5m of retrofit funding and leaves the council with unencumbered ownership of the homes when the lease ends.

That last point is the whole game. A conventional private sector lease ends with the keys handed back, a hand-back negotiation and a fresh deal at whatever the market rent is in 2036. The council has paid for ten years of supply and has nothing on its balance sheet to show for it.

Not every council can or should do a 42-year deal. But every council signing a ten-year lease should be able to show it has compared the alternatives: buying, institutional funding that ends in ownership, or recycling value from stock it already holds. On that last option, Westminster's £50m, 40-year sale-and-leaseback with the City of Westminster Pension Fund released capital from 124 existing TA homes to fund new supply.

Subsidy still does not follow the cost

Whatever the model, the income side has not moved. DWP's housing benefit subsidy guidance limits subsidy on leased and licensed temporary accommodation to 90% of the January 2011 Local Housing Allowance rate. The District Councils' Network has put the resulting gap at £268m a year for district councils alone.

A cheaper lease narrows that gap. It does not close it. And unlike a nightly placement, you cannot walk away from a lease when the arithmetic changes.

Prevention has to count as investment

There is a wider policy opening. The Treasury plans to introduce preventative departmental expenditure limits at the 2027 Spending Review, as Lígia Teixeira of the Centre for Homelessness Impact set out in Inside Housing on 11 November. She notes that English councils spent £4bn gross on homelessness services last year, much of it responding to crises that had already happened.

If the new framework is to mean anything, long-term TA supply that takes families out of nightly paid rooms should count as invest-to-save, not crisis spending. Councils that can evidence the savings will be best placed to make that case.

What this means for councils

  • Treat long leases as capital decisions. Bring them into the capital strategy and prudential indicators, and test them against your borrowing limits before you sign.
  • Always run the ownership comparison. Lease, acquire, institutional funding ending in ownership, or releasing value from existing stock. Compare whole-life cost and the position at the end of the term, not just year-one savings.
  • Read the end of the lease first. Hand-back conditions, break clauses, rent reviews and renewal rights decide what the deal really costs.
  • Model the subsidy gap for the full term. Assume the 2011 freeze stays unless policy changes.
  • Prepare for the Spending Review. Build the evidence that long-term supply is prevention, because that is the case the new preventative budgets will need.

Moving away from nightly paid accommodation is the right direction. Just make sure the deal you sign today is one your successor will thank you for in ten years.
